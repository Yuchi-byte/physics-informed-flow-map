The Likelihood Scale Problem

MSE data misfit, the noise scale σ, and why every steering method renormalizes its gradient · 2026-06-27 · working note — for review & discussion

Working note · not yet in the curated set Prior work · Research plan · Index

When we steer a learned prior toward seismic data, we add a gradient of a data-misfit reward $r(v)=-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert F(v)-d\rVert^2$. MFM's theory says the exact value-gradient steer — with this reward and a calibrated σ — samples the exact Bayesian posterior, with no renormalization. Yet that exact, renorm-free steer is precisely what blows up in our runs, and the empirical fix (gradient renormalization) leaves the exact theory. This note traces the misfit to its assumptions, explains why the exact estimator explodes (variance and stiffness, not a wrong magnitude), and asks whether we can stabilize it without abandoning exactness.

1 · The tension

The Meta-Flow-Map steered SDE (paper Eq. 38) is exact: with the soft value $V_t(x)=\log\mathbb{E}_{p_0}[\exp r(X_1)\mid X_t{=}x]$, $$dX^\* = \big[b_t + \tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\nabla\log p_t + \sigma_t^2\nabla V_t\big]dt + \sigma_t\,dB$$ samples exactly $p_1^\*\propto p_1\exp(r)$. With $r=-\lVert F(v)-d\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)=\log\mathcal N(d\mid F(v),\sigma^2 I)$, that is the exact Bayesian posterior $p(v\mid d)$ — and the steering magnitude $\sigma_t^2\nabla V_t$ is derived, not free. σ here is a physical quantity: the measurement-noise std. In principle, calibrate σ and you sample the right posterior. In practice, no value of σ is simultaneously physically calibrated and usable:

The problem: the exact, renorm-free steer at a calibrated σ is what we want — but the estimator of $\nabla V_t$ is too high-variance to run there, and the only σ that is stable without renorm (≈1000) is too weak to steer. renorm buys stability by abandoning exactness. This note is about stabilizing the estimator without that trade.

2 · Where the MSE misfit comes from

The least-squares data misfit is not a "loss" someone designed; it is a negative log-likelihood. Model the data residual as additive noise,

$$d_{\text{obs}} = F(m_\star) + \eta, \qquad \eta\sim\mathcal N(0, C_D),$$

and the likelihood is $p(d\mid m)\propto\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12 (F(m)-d)^\top C_D^{-1}(F(m)-d)\big)$, so its negative log is the Mahalanobis misfit

$$S(m)=\tfrac12\,(F(m)-d)^\top C_D^{-1}(F(m)-d).$$

Plain mean-squared error is the special case $C_D=\sigma^2 I$:

$$S(m)=\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\lVert F(m)-d\rVert_2^2.$$

This lineage is concrete. Gauss (1809) tied least squares to the normal error law; Tarantola & Valette (1982) recast it as maximum-likelihood estimation under Gaussian data uncertainties; Tarantola (1984) applied that generalized least-squares criterion to seismic waveforms via the adjoint-state gradient — the paper now credited as the origin of FWI; and Inverse Problem Theory (1987/2005) contains the precise statement that the $C_D^{-1}$-weighted misfit reduces to MSE exactly when $C_D=\sigma^2 I$. Taufik & Alkhalifah (2026) state the same reduction verbatim for the diffusion-FWI setting.

The assumptions, stated baldly. Writing the misfit as $\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert F(v)-d\rVert^2$ — exactly our reward — asserts that the residual $F(v)-d$ is, across all $N=5\times70\times1001=350{,}350$ shot-gather samples:
  • zero-mean (no systematic modeling bias),
  • Gaussian,
  • independent across every time sample, receiver, and source ($C_D$ diagonal),
  • identically scaled — one variance $\sigma^2$ for all 350k entries (stationary).
σ is, by this construction, the per-measurement noise standard deviation. Nothing more, nothing less. Every departure of σ from that meaning (see §3) is a departure from the model.

3 · σ has always been a step knob

Here is the reassuring part: treating σ as a tunable scale rather than a measured noise level is not our sin — it is how FWI has always worked. The standard review (Virieux & Operto, 2009) writes the objective as $C(m)=\tfrac12\,\Delta d^\dagger\Delta d$ — that is, $C_D=I$, the covariance simply dropped. A data covariance reappears only as a design weight $W_d$ (e.g. an offset gain), never as a calibrated noise std. Any overall scalar out front is absorbed by the line-search step length, and where a model prior is added, the data-vs-prior trade-off weight ε plays the role of $\sigma^2$ and is tuned by the L-curve (Hansen, 1998), not measured.

So in deterministic FWI, only the relative weighting moves the minimizer; the absolute misfit scale is a step-size choice. Our σ is the same knob, inherited.

The √N bridge — why σ≈100 is the only renorm-free value that does anything. Our reward sums residuals over $N=350{,}350$ samples without averaging. The same reward written in sum form ($r=-S/2\sigma_s^2$, $S=\sum_i r_i^2$) and mean form ($r=-M/2\sigma_m^2$, $M=S/N$) are identical iff $$\sigma_s=\sqrt{N}\,\sigma_m,\qquad \sqrt{N}\approx 592.$$ A physically plausible per-sample scale is $\sigma_m\sim 0.17$ (the data has std ≈ 1.5, abs-max ≈ 43, so sub-unity per-sample noise is reasonable). Plug in: $\sigma_s\approx 592\times0.17\approx 100$. The working "σ = 100" is not a noise level — it is $\sqrt{N}$ times a sane per-sample scale. σ=100 is secretly performing the mean-normalization through the temperature, which is precisely why it is the one renorm-free value that steers without exploding (σ≈1) or going inert (σ≈1000). The un-normalized sum is the hidden factor $N$ that distorts everything.

4 · L2 is a convenience, not a truth

Before we work hard to "do the likelihood correctly," it is worth being honest that the Gaussian-i.i.d. model is itself a convenience for waveform data. Real seismic residuals are correlated across adjacent traces, non-stationary, and dominated not by random noise but by coherent modeling error (wrong starting model, missing low frequencies, approximate physics). L2 also compares wiggles sample-by-sample with no notion of time-shift, which makes the misfit non-convex and prone to cycle-skipping (Bunks et al., 1995). That pathology is the entire reason the field built transport-aware misfits — optimal transport / Wasserstein (Engquist & Froese, 2014), envelope, AWI (Warner & Guasch, 2016).

discussWhat we inherit. By steering with $-\lVert F(v)-d\rVert^2$ we adopt the Gaussian convenience and its non-convexity. In our favour: we operate on simulated, noiseless, inverse-crime data, so the Gaussian model is "true" by construction and cycle-skipping is mild at our band. Against us: that same setting is exactly where a calibrated σ is least informative (the residual at the true model is ~0), which is part of why the principled and heuristic scalings are hard to tell apart empirically. Open question: do we ever want to swap the L2 reward for a transport-aware one, or is the prior doing that job?

5 · Why the exact, renorm-free steer explodes

It is tempting to say "the literal $\sigma^{-2}$ magnitude is just wrong." For DPS that is roughly true; for MFM-G it is not, and the distinction is the whole point.

5.1 · DPS is biased; MFM-G is unbiased but high-variance

DPS replaces the intractable likelihood with a point estimate — the Tweedie posterior mean $\hat v=\mathbb E[v\mid x_t]$ plugged into $F$. Because $F$ is nonlinear, $F(\mathbb E[v])\neq\mathbb E[F(v)]$ (a Jensen gap), so the $\sigma^{-2}$ weight derived for the true posterior no longer multiplies the right object. DPS's own fix — normalize the gradient and tune a step — concedes this: it keeps the direction, discards the magnitude. MFM-G is categorically different: $\nabla V_t=\nabla\log\tfrac1K\sum_i\exp r(\hat X_1^i)$ over genuine posterior draws $\hat X_1^i=X(0,1,\epsilon^i\mid t_{\text{cond}},x_{\text{cond}})$ is a consistent estimator of the exact value gradient (as $K\to\infty$). There is no Jensen / point-estimate bias of the DPS kind to discard. So the magnitude is correct in the limit — the failure is finite-$K$ estimation (§5.2), not a wrong target.

5.2 · The logsumexp estimate collapses to one-hot at small σ

$\nabla V_t$ is a softmax-weighted average of per-draw reward gradients, weights $w_i\propto\exp(r_i)$, $r_i=-S_i/2\sigma^2$. As σ shrinks, the spread of $r_i$ across the $K$ draws grows like $1/\sigma^2$, the softmax collapses onto whichever single draw fit best, and $\nabla V_t\approx\nabla r(\hat X_1^{i^\*})$ — a huge, high-variance estimate that depends on which of our K=4 random draws won. (Strictly: the finite-$K$ logsumexp is a biased estimator of $\log\mathbb E[\exp r]$ — consistent only as $K\to\infty$ — so at K=4 there is both bias and variance; the variance is what makes it explode.) This is the explosion: an estimator failure, not a wrong weight.

5.3 · The un-normalized sum drives the collapse early

$S=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\text{res}_i^2$ over $N{=}350{,}350$ samples makes the across-draw spread of $r_i$ a factor $\sim N$ larger, so the softmax goes one-hot at a far milder σ than a per-sample reward would. The §3 √N story, reread: its real damage is to the estimator's variance, not to a magnitude. Mean-normalizing ($S/N$) is therefore variance control, not magnitude control.

5.4 · Stiffness is a separate, estimator-free failure

Even with the exact $\nabla V_t$ (infinite $K$), a tiny σ makes the true posterior extremely concentrated, so the exact drift is genuinely large and a finite Euler–Maruyama step cannot track it. Discretization diverges independently of estimation — a stiffness problem cured by smaller steps or σ-annealing, not by more samples. This is MFM's own stated reason for renorm (F.3.6): with large MFM-G drifts they "would require a much finer time discretisation… to avoid excessive discretisation error," so they rescale instead, accepting a bias that is "only in the magnitude of the drift and not the direction." So renorm is a deliberate stiffness workaround, not an unprincipled hack — though it does not distinguish §5.2 variance spikes from genuine §5.4 stiffness; it caps both.

6 · How everyone controls the magnitude

No inference-time method runs the literal, calibrated $\sigma^{-2}$ magnitude. But the reason differs, and MFM is not in the same boat as the rest — a distinction the first draft of this note got wrong.

DPS, RED-DiffEq, diffefwi have no exact-magnitude to begin with. Their likelihood is a biased point approximation (§5.1), so normalizing the gradient and tuning a step is the method, not a departure from it. MFM is the exception: its value-gradient SDE is exact (§1), so it does have a derived magnitude — and renorm_gradient is an optional stabilizer (F.3.6) that biases that magnitude to keep the SDE trackable at a coarse discretization. MFM frames it as a discretization fix and is explicit the bias is "only in the magnitude… not the direction." The three normalize because they must; MFM renormalizes to tame large drifts — whether from §5.4 stiffness or §5.2 estimator spikes, it caps both.

Two renorm variants — and we only tried the aggressive one. F.3.6 offers rescaling (pin steering to $\lambda\lVert b_t\rVert$ always) and clipping (cap at $\lambda\lVert b_t\rVert$, leave the exact magnitude untouched below it). MFM used rescaling with $\lambda{=}1$ for ImageNet, and our renorm=True inherits exactly that default — so we have been running their published method, not a homemade one. Clipping is the untried, better-fitting option for us: it preserves the exact $\sigma_t^2\nabla V_t$ whenever it is not exploding and intervenes only on the spikes — "exact-when-safe," which hands σ back its role as the real knob.

The "why" for the first three: (i) the biased point-estimate breaks the Bayesian scaling anyway (§5.1), and (ii) a normalized step is a stable single-scalar knob. For MFM the magnitude is correct in expectation, so renorm treats a symptom — large drift — that has two distinct causes (stiffness, §5.4; estimator variance, §5.2). Clipping treats it more surgically than rescaling by leaving the in-range magnitude alone.

keyΠGDM points at the variance-aware middle ground. It replaces a constant σ with a time-varying denoising uncertainty $r_t^2=\sigma_t^2/(1+\sigma_t^2)$ — small steps when the posterior is broad and the estimate noisy, full data weight when it sharpens. For MFM-G this is suggestive rather than a drop-in: our $r_t^2$ is not assumed but measurable from the spread of the posterior draws we already compute (§7).

7 · Constructive proposal & experiments

The reframing from §5 changes the goal. We are not looking for a better magnitude to replace renorm — the magnitude is already correct in expectation. We want to run the exact estimator at a calibrated σ by reducing its variance and stiffness, so that renorm becomes unnecessary rather than load-bearing. The three failure modes (§5.2–5.4) each have a fix that keeps the SDE exact.

Keep the exact reward; fix the estimator. Keep $r(v)=-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma_m^2}\,\tfrac1N\lVert F(v)-d\rVert^2$ (i.e. $C_D=\sigma_m^2 I$, mean-normalized) — still an exact Gaussian likelihood — and attack the three mechanisms directly:
  1. Mean-normalize (the $1/N$) so the across-draw spread of $r_i$ drops by $N$ and the softmax stays off one-hot — kills §5.3, makes the useful $\sigma_m$ land at $O(1)$.
  2. Variance-reduce the logsumexp: raise $K$, and subtract a per-step baseline / control variate inside $\log\tfrac1K\sum_i\exp(r_i-\bar r)$ so the softmax weights are well-conditioned — directly attacks §5.2 without touching the magnitude.
  3. Anneal σ along $t$ (large early, calibrated late), the stiffness cure of §5.4 — and exactly the behavior ΠGDM's $r_t^2$ encodes. Better still, set it from the measured spread of the posterior draws we already compute, rather than assuming it.
renorm=True short-circuits all three by capping the drift to $\lVert b_t\rVert$ — cheap, but it stops sampling $p_1\exp(r)$. The bet is that (1)+(2)+(3) make the renorm-free, σ-calibrated steer actually runnable.
discussThe bet, and what would falsify it. Hypothesis: a variance-reduced, mean-normalized, σ-annealed MFM-G runs renorm-free at a calibrated σ and recovers the exact posterior — beating MFM's $\text{gs}\cdot\lVert b_t\rVert$ on calibration/SSIM at matched data-fit. Experiments, cheapest first:
  • E1 — mean-normalize alone. renorm=False, misfit $/N$, sweep $\sigma_m\in\{0.3,1,3\}$. Prediction: the explosion softens and the useful σ collapses from ~100 to $O(1)$. Do first.
  • E2 — variance reduction. Hold $\sigma_m$ fixed, sweep $K\in\{4,16,64\}$ and add a baseline. Prediction: at small $\sigma_m$ the divergence is a variance effect — it recedes as $K$ grows. This is the direct test of the §5 thesis.
  • E3 — σ-anneal / measured $r_t^2$. Schedule σ down over $t$. Prediction: stiffness-driven blow-up disappears even at K=4.
  • E4 — measurement noise, σ matched. The only setting where data-fit and model-accuracy stop being adversaries, and the only honest test of a calibrated σ. Needs the Evaluator noise_std path.
Falsifier: if E2 shows the small-$\sigma_m$ blow-up is insensitive to $K$, then it is stiffness (§5.4), not estimator variance — and the §5 thesis is wrong about which mechanism dominates.
caveatHonest counter-position. On noiseless inverse-crime FlatVel-A, DPS already wins (MAE 0.243, half the solves) because data-fitting dominates and a biased-but-cheap Tweedie step is enough. A variance-reduced, renorm-free MFM-G steer may only show its edge once (a) there is real measurement noise, (b) the posterior is genuinely multi-modal, or (c) calibration is scored — i.e. exactly the regimes the research plan already bets on. This note does not claim renorm-free steering wins on today's benchmark; it claims it is the principled version, and tells us where to look for the win.
