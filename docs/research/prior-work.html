Generative Priors & Flow Matching for Full-Waveform Inversion

Prior-work review · compiled 2026-06-25 · physics-informed-flow-map

Prior work · 1 / 2 Index · Next: Research plan →

Full-waveform inversion (FWI) recovers a subsurface velocity map from seismic recordings by fitting the wave equation to data. It is the canonical large-scale, non-convex, ill-posed PDE inverse problem. This page traces prior work from the classical optimization formulation, through supervised deep learning, to the modern wave of generative priors (diffusion and flow matching), and locates where this project — a flow-map prior with physics-residual training — fits.

The three generative camps (the spine of section 6). A learn a prior $p(v)$ offline, steer it with the wave equation at inference (DPS, RED-DiffEq).   B learn a conditional map seismic→velocity in one pass (DiffusionVel, rectified flow, I2SB).   C bake PDE residuals into training of the generative model (PIDM, PBFM). Camp C for FWI is the open gap this project targets.

1 · The full-waveform inversion problem

1.1 Physical setup & forward operator

Seismic sources at the surface emit waves that propagate through the subsurface; receivers record the resulting wavefields as time series (seismograms). The goal is to reconstruct the spatial velocity model $m(\mathbf{x})$ (and sometimes density / elastic parameters) that governs how waves travel. The forward physics is the wave equation — in the acoustic, constant-density approximation:

$$\frac{1}{c(\mathbf{x})^2}\,\partial_{tt}\,p(\mathbf{x},t) \;-\; \nabla^2 p(\mathbf{x},t) \;=\; s(\mathbf{x},t),$$

with $c=\sqrt{1/m}$ the wave speed and $s$ the source. The full elastic case adds shear waves via the Navier equation with Lamé parameters. Abstractly, the forward operator maps a model to predicted data, $\mathbf{d}=F(\mathbf{m})$ — well-posed but expensive (one PDE solve per source). FWI was introduced by Tarantola (1984) and Lailly (1983).

1.2 The inverse problem & why it is hard

FWI minimizes a data misfit over the model:

$$\min_{\mathbf{m}}\;\tfrac12\,\big\|F(\mathbf{m})-\mathbf{d}_{\text{obs}}\big\|^2 .$$

  • Computational cost. Each gradient needs forward + adjoint PDE solves for every source, at every iteration — the dominant expense.
  • Non-convexity / cycle skipping. Oscillatory data makes the misfit riddled with local minima; if the predicted phase is more than half a wavelength off, optimization locks onto a wrong basin (Virieux & Operto 2009).
  • Ill-posedness / under-determination. Limited aperture and bandwidth leave many models consistent with the data — a prior/regularizer is essential.

These three pressures — cost, non-convexity, ill-posedness — are exactly what learned priors aim to relieve.

2 · Classical (optimization-based) FWI

2.1 The adjoint-state gradient & optimization

The enabling trick is the adjoint-state method (Plessix 2006; Tarantola 1984): the gradient of the misfit is the zero-lag cross-correlation of the forward wavefield with a back-propagated adjoint wavefield, giving the full gradient at the cost of ~two PDE solves per source (independent of the number of model parameters). The model is then updated with steepest descent, nonlinear conjugate gradients, L-BFGS, or Gauss–Newton / truncated-Newton schemes (Pratt et al. 1998; Métivier et al. 2017).

2.2 Cycle skipping & multi-scale strategies

The standard defense against local minima is frequency continuation: invert low frequencies first (smooth, convex-ish), then progressively add higher frequencies (Bunks et al. 1995; Sirgue & Pratt 2004). This demands a good starting model and low-frequency data — often unavailable in practice, which is precisely the regime where a strong generative prior helps most.

2.3 Regularization

Because the problem is under-determined, classical FWI adds priors as penalties: Tikhonov ($L^2$ smoothness, ≡ a Gaussian prior) and total-variation (TV) (edge-preserving, ≡ a Laplacian prior; the ROF model of Rudin–Osher–Fatemi 1992, brought to FWI by Lin & Huang 2015). These hand-designed priors are exactly what a learned generative prior $p(\mathbf{m})$ replaces with a data-driven one.

2.4 Misfit-function engineering

An orthogonal line attacks cycle skipping by changing the loss, not the model: optimal-transport / Wasserstein misfits (Engquist & Froese 2014; Métivier et al. 2016), envelope inversion (Wu et al. 2014), and adaptive waveform inversion (Warner & Guasch 2016) convexify the objective. Note the recurring OT theme — it reappears as straight-path transport in flow matching (§5).

2.5 Computational tricks

Source encoding / simultaneous sources (Krebs et al. 2009) and stochastic / mini-batch FWI amortize the per-source PDE cost — the classical analog of stochastic optimization in deep learning.

Takeaway. Classical FWI = expensive PDE-constrained optimization with hand-built regularizers and starting models. Its three pain points (cost, local minima, ill-posedness) motivate everything that follows: learn the prior, or learn the inverse map, from data.

3 · Supervised deep learning

The first ML wave learned a direct seismic→velocity map with a CNN, trained on paired synthetic data:

  • InversionNet (Wu & Lin 2020) — encoder–decoder CNN, the standard supervised baseline.
  • VelocityGAN (Zhang & Lin 2022) — adversarially-trained inversion for sharper outputs.

OpenFWI (Deng et al., NeurIPS 2022) is the community benchmark: 12 families of paired (seismic, velocity) data — FlatVel, CurveVel (layered), FlatFault, CurveFault (faulted), Style (realistic), CO2 — scored by MAE, RMSE, and SSIM on the velocity map. This project trains its prior on the OpenFWI velocity maps. Limitations of pure supervision: needs large paired datasets, gives a single point estimate (no uncertainty), and generalizes poorly out-of-distribution — which generative methods address.

4 · Benchmarks & datasets

Ground-truth velocity is unknowable in the field, so FWI-ML methods are trained and scored almost entirely on synthetic (velocity, seismic) pairs — the choice of benchmark therefore defines what "works" means. One dataset dominates the recent generative literature, OpenFWI, and this project trains its prior on its velocity maps. It is worth setting out exactly what that data covers, how it is made, how far it sits from reality, and what each camp actually trains and evaluates on.

4.1 OpenFWI — coverage

OpenFWI (Deng et al. 2022) is a collection of 12 datasets (≈2.1 TB with seismic) in four families: Vel and Fault (analytic layered models), Style (style-transfer textures), and a 3D Kimberlina CO₂-reservoir family. The ten 2D Vel/Fault/Style datasets — the ones the generative-prior literature uses, and the ones staged in this repo — total ≈470k velocity maps on a uniform 70×70 grid at 10 m spacing, velocities 1500–4500 m/s. Each family splits into an A (easier) and B (harder) tier by structural complexity. We pulled velocity maps only (~8.8 GB); the seismic counterpart — 5 surface Ricker shots (≈15 Hz) × 70 receivers × 1000 samples at 1 ms, the acquisition our Deepwave operator replicates — runs to hundreds of GB and is regenerated on demand.

4.2 How the velocity maps are generated

OpenFWI builds velocity maps from three kinds of prior, then forward-models the seismic data from each map with a 2D acoustic solver:

  • Mathematical / procedural (Vel, Fault) — start from a layered baseline (velocity generally increasing with depth) and recursively apply equations to bend layers into folds (Curve) and insert fault throws (Fault). Piecewise-smooth models with clean, sharp interfaces.
  • Style transfer (Style) — a style-transfer network paints velocity structure using natural images as the style source, giving richer textures. The realism is visual, not geological: the style images are not subsurface data.
  • Reservoir simulation (Kimberlina) — physically simulated CO₂-storage models from DOE's NRAP program; the only family grounded in a real geological scenario (and the only 3D one).

This project learns an unconditional prior $p(v)$ over exactly these maps — so the generative process above is the distribution the prior tries to approximate.

4.3 How representative is this of the real subsurface?

OpenFWI is a controlled ML benchmark, not a field surrogate. The 2D families lack salt bodies, clinoforms, and velocity interbedding; Style's realism is style-transfer from non-geological images; the domain is a tiny ~0.7 km 2D patch; and the physics is the simplified case — 2D acoustic, constant density, a single known source wavelet, noiseless data (our own forward operator inherits all of these). Field FWI additionally contends with elasticity, anisotropy, attenuation, 3D propagation, unknown wavelets, and noise. The community treats this synthetic-to-field gap as an open problem: topping OpenFWI does not imply field transfer — which is why more realistic benchmarks exist (GeoFWI, field-scale SubsurfaceGen, and the classical Marmousi / SEAM / Overthrust / BP-salt models, each with its own diversity limits).

For this project that is a statement of scope, not validity: an OpenFWI result is a clean methodology proof-of-concept (controlled, ground-truthed), not a field-validated claim. Usefully, the inference-time half of a camp-A method is the transferable part — the wave-equation gradient is exact for any geology, so the machinery would carry to a more realistic setting by swapping the prior. The prior is the component tied to OpenFWI's stylized distribution.

4.4 What prior works train and evaluate on

The benchmark protocol is per-dataset: train on a dataset's training split, test on its held-out split, and report MAE, RMSE, and SSIM on the velocity map (the metrics in the §3 table). The training scope, though, splits sharply by camp:

  • AB Per-family is the norm. Generative-prior and conditional methods train one model per dataset: DiffusionVel trains a separate diffusion model for each B-family; RED-DiffEq trains an OpenFWI prior and then tests zero-shot on Marmousi/Overthrust. This keeps numbers comparable to the leaderboard.
  • B Pooling is the "large-model" exception — and it is supervised. Direct seismic→velocity regressors do pool families: FWI in the large-model era trains on all ten (408k pairs, augmented to ~5M), and an empirical study finds combined training improves MAE ≈13% with stronger leave-one-out generalization. That evidence is for the conditional map, not a learned prior.

5 · Background: diffusion & flow-matching generative models

5.1 Score-based diffusion

Diffusion models corrupt data with a forward SDE and learn to reverse it via the score $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p_t(\mathbf{x})$ (Song et al. 2021). The same marginals admit a deterministic probability-flow ODE — the conceptual bridge to flow matching.

5.2 Flow matching

Flow Matching (Lipman et al. 2023) trains a velocity field $v_\theta(\mathbf{x},t)$ by regressing it against an analytic conditional velocity along a fixed probability path — simulation-free. With the Gaussian optimal-transport path $\mathbf{x}_t=(1-t)\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+t\,\mathbf{x}_1$, trajectories are nearly straight, so few ODE steps (NFEs) suffice at sampling:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\big\|v_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t)-u_t(\mathbf{x}_t\mid \mathbf{x}_1)\big\|^2,\qquad \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}=v_\theta(\mathbf{x},t).$$

Rectified Flow (Liu et al. 2023) makes the paths literally straight (regress against $\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_0$) and "reflows" to reach 1–2-step generation; it underpins SD3/FLUX (Esser et al. 2024).

5.3 Stochastic interpolants — the unifying view

Stochastic Interpolants (Albergo, Boffi & Vanden-Eijnden 2023) write $\mathbf{x}_t=\alpha(t)\mathbf{x}_0+\beta(t)\mathbf{x}_1+\gamma(t)\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, recovering flow matching ($\gamma{=}0$, linear $\alpha,\beta$) and diffusion (VP schedules) as special cases. The mfm reference package in this repo implements the linear interpolant.

5.4 Consistency models & flow maps

To sample in one/few steps, consistency models (Song et al. 2023) learn the flow map to $t{=}0$; flow maps (Boffi et al. 2025) generalize to map between arbitrary times $\Phi(\mathbf{x}_s,s,t)$. Meta Flow Maps (Potaptchik et al., ICML 2026 — the mfm package here) make flow maps stochastic: one-step i.i.d. draws of the clean sample from any intermediate state, which is exactly the machinery this project uses as its generative prior. (Distinct from the similarly-named Meta Flow Matching, Atanackovic et al. 2024, which amortizes over initial distributions.)

5.5 Meta Flow Maps in depth — the stochastic posterior this project steers

The mfm package is the engine for our inference, so it is worth stating precisely what it learns and the inference-time machinery it unlocks. (Equation numbers below refer to Potaptchik et al. 2026.)

Model. A meta flow map is an average-velocity field $v_\theta(s,u,\mathbf{x},t_{\text{cond}},\mathbf{x}_{\text{cond}})$ that transports a state at time $s$ to time $u$ in a single step via the map $X(s,u,\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}+(u-s)\,v_\theta$. On the diagonal $s{=}u$ it reduces to the ordinary flow-matching velocity $v_t$; off the diagonal it is a genuine few-step map. The extra arguments $(t_{\text{cond}},\mathbf{x}_{\text{cond}})$ are the conditioning channel: the map is told it has already observed a noised state $\mathbf{x}_{\text{cond}}=\mathbf{x}_{t_{\text{cond}}}$ at level $t_{\text{cond}}$, so it learns the conditional posterior $p_{1|t_{\text{cond}}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_{\text{cond}})$ over clean data, not just the marginal.

Training. Two terms (both implemented in mfm/losses/losses.py). (i) A diagonal flow-matching loss regresses $v_\theta(s,s,\cdot)$ against $\mathbf{x}_1-\mathbf{x}_0$. (ii) An off-diagonal self-consistency ("mutual-flow") loss enforces that the few-step map agrees with its own instantaneous velocity, regressing $v_\theta(s,u,\cdot)$ against the tangent target $v_{ss}+(u-s)\,\partial_s X$ — a directional derivative computed in one forward pass with a Jacobian-vector product (torch.func.jvp). The key practical point: in the mf regime this target is built from the model and the data alone, so the flow map is trained from scratch with no teacher (GLASS distillation from a pretrained DMF is an option, not a requirement). A warm-up trains the diagonal first, then the jump size $u-s$ and the conditioning level $t_{\text{cond}}$ are annealed open.

The payoff — one-step posterior sampling. From any state $\mathbf{x}_t$, set $t_{\text{cond}}{=}t,\ \mathbf{x}_{\text{cond}}{=}\mathbf{x}_t$; then each fresh noise draw $\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ gives a clean sample $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_1=\boldsymbol\epsilon+v_\theta(0,1,\boldsymbol\epsilon,t,\mathbf{x}_t) =\Phi(\boldsymbol\epsilon;t,\mathbf{x}_t)\sim p_{1|t}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_t)$ in one network evaluation. Many i.i.d. posterior draws, no inner ODE/SDE rollout — this is what consistency models and ordinary flow maps cannot give, and it is the whole reason MFM exists.

Why a posterior sampler is the missing piece for steering. To bias a generator toward a reward $r$ (for us, data fit) the optimally controlled process adds the gradient of a value function to the drift (Doob's $h$-transform):

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}^\star_t=b_t(\mathbf{x}^\star_t)+\tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\,\nabla V_t(\mathbf{x}^\star_t), \qquad V_t(\mathbf{x})=\log\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{r(\mathbf{x}_1)}\mid \mathbf{x}_t=\mathbf{x}\right].\tag{16,19}$$

The base drift $b_t$ is free from the pretrained model; the only hard part is estimating $\nabla V_t$, which is an expectation over exactly the posterior $p_{1|t}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_t)$ the flow map now samples cheaply. The paper gives two consistent Monte-Carlo estimators:

  • MFM-GF (gradient-free, Eq. 20) — a self-normalised, reward-weighted average of posterior draws $\sum_i \hat{\mathbf{x}}_1^{(i)}e^{r^{(i)}}\!/\!\sum_i e^{r^{(i)}}$. Needs only reward evaluations, so it handles non-differentiable rewards, but scales poorly with dimension and needs many draws.
  • MFM-G (gradient-based, Eq. 22) — the reparametrised estimator $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log\frac1N\sum_i e^{\,r(\Phi(\boldsymbol\epsilon^{(i)};t,\mathbf{x}))}$, i.e. the IWAE log-mean-exp bound (Burda et al. 2016) differentiated through both the reward and the one-step posterior map. It requires a differentiable reward and posterior map, and in the paper it substantially outperforms MFM-GF, DPS, and Best-of-$N$ — even the single-draw ($N{=}1$) variant beats the baselines.

For contrast, plain DPS-style guidance uses the single Tweedie posterior mean $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_1=\mathbf{x}_t+(1-t)\,v_t$ in place of genuine draws — exact only when the reward is linear, and biased by a Jensen gap otherwise. MFM-G is precisely the upgrade that removes that bias when posterior draws are available.

What this means for our FWI tilting. Our reward is the data log-likelihood $r(\mathbf{v})=-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert F(\mathbf{v})-\mathbf{d}\rVert^2$, and $F$ is the differentiable Deepwave wave solver — so $\nabla r$ is available and MFM-G is the estimator we use (the gradient-free MFM-GF is a fallback for non-differentiable misfits). Concretely this replaces the biased Tweedie-mean misfit gradient in DPS with the gradient of $\log\frac1N\sum_i\exp\!\big(\!-\lVert F(\hat{\mathbf{v}}^{(i)})-\mathbf{d}\rVert^2/2\sigma^2\big)$ over $N$ genuine posterior draws $\hat{\mathbf{v}}^{(i)}\sim p(\mathbf{v}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)$. The win is posterior quality (less-biased likelihood + calibrated uncertainty from the spread of draws), not cost: each step now costs $N$ forward+adjoint wave solves instead of one. This is the camp-A inference upgrade this project is building toward; see the research plan.

6 · Generative models for FWI — the three camps

A  Unconditional prior + inference-time guidance

Train a prior $p(\mathbf{m})$ on velocity models offline; at solve time, steer it toward data consistency using the wave-equation misfit. The foundation is Diffusion Posterior Sampling (Chung et al. 2023), which injects a likelihood gradient at each reverse step. In FWI:

  • RED-DiffEq (Shan, Zhu, Lin & Lu, Commun. Phys. 2026) — the paper that prompted this review. An OpenFWI-trained diffusion model used as a regularization-by-denoising term in the FWI objective ($\text{data misfit}+\lambda\cdot\text{denoiser residual}$); not retrained. Trained on OpenFWI, it generalizes zero-shot to Marmousi and Overthrust.
  • Prior-regularized FWI (Wang et al. 2023), Robust Physics-Guided Diffusion (Wasserstein-2 guidance, 2026), and Bayesian/UQ work from the SLIM group (WISER, Yin et al. 2024).

Strength: principled posterior & uncertainty, no paired data. Cost: a wave-equation solve every guidance step.

B  Conditional / amortized (seismic → velocity)

Learn a conditional generator; the wave equation only makes the training set, and inference is one fast pass. Dominant on OpenFWI leaderboards.

  • DiffusionVel (Zhang, Li & Huang 2024) — ensemble of conditional + unconditional diffusion; best published conditional OpenFWI numbers (table in §3). Notable for multi-information integration — see §6.D.
  • Conditional Rectified Flow (Xu et al. 2026) and the I2SB Schrödinger bridge (Stankevich & Petrov 2025, code available, all 10 families) — the flow-matching entrants, closest in machinery to this project's prior; straight-path transport gives few-NFE inference.
  • Geological/well-prior conditional diffusion with classifier-free guidance (Wang et al. 2024).

Strength: fast, benchmark-friendly. Weakness: amortization gap, large paired data, OOD-fragile, point-ish estimates.

C  Physics residual in training

Bake the PDE/constraint residual into the generative model's training objective, so samples are physically consistent by construction:

  • PIDM — Physics-Informed Diffusion Models (Bastek, Sun & Kochmann, ICLR 2025) — adds $c_{\text{res}}\,\|R(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0)\|^2$ on the Tweedie-denoised estimate to the diffusion loss. The PhysicsInformedDiffusionModels reference package here.
  • PBFM — Physics-Based Flow Matching (Baldan, Liu, Guardone & Thuerey, ICLR 2026) — the flow-matching analog of PIDM: PDE residuals in the FM objective, with conflict-free gradient balancing (ConFIG) and temporal unrolling to close the Jensen gap. Up to 8× lower residual error vs. FM/PIDM on Darcy & Kolmogorov flow. The PBFM reference package here.
Camp C for FWI is essentially unexplored. PIDM/PBFM demonstrate physics-in-training on fluid/material PDEs, not on seismic FWI / OpenFWI velocity maps. Almost every FWI generative paper puts physics at inference (camp A) or nowhere (camp B). That intersection — physics-residual training of a flow-matching FWI prior — is the opening this project targets.

6.D  Incorporating heterogeneous knowledge (multiple measurement types)

Real surveys carry more than one observation: seismic shot gathers, sparse but accurate well logs, a smooth background/migration velocity, gravity, and qualitative geological priors. A recent line fuses these into one generative inversion:

  • DiffusionVel (Zhang, Li & Huang 2024) trains three separate diffusion models — a seismic-conditional GDM, a well-log-conditional GDM, and an unconditional geology prior — plus a low-pass background-velocity term, and combines their clean-sample predictions by weighted averaging at sampling time (their Eqs. 16–18, manual weights $\lambda_k$). The fusion is post-hoc: at inference you mix-and-match sources or retune $\lambda_k$ without retraining.
  • Geological- and well-prior conditional diffusion (Wang, Huang & Alkhalifah 2024) instead fuses geology-class labels and well data through classifier-free guidance weights.
The takeaway for a posterior view. Multiple measurements are simply multiple likelihood factors: $\;p(v\mid d_{\text{seis}},d_{\text{well}},\dots)\;\propto\;p(v)\prod_k \exp\!\big(-\tfrac1{2\sigma_k^2}\|F_k(v)-d_k\|^2\big)$. DiffusionVel approximates this by hand-weighted averaging of per-source models; a tilting framework can instead add one tilt per source — a more principled (and retraining-free) fusion. This is a direct extension of this project's plan (see the research-plan's multi-measurement direction).

7 · Flow matching for inverse problems

Because flow models lack an explicit score, the diffusion posterior-sampling machinery had to be re-derived for them — directly relevant to a future conditional $p(\mathbf{v}\mid \mathbf{d})$ for FWI:

  • FlowDPS (Kim et al., ICCV 2025) — a flow-version of Tweedie's formula enabling training-free posterior sampling with flow priors.
  • Flow Priors for inverse problems (Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2024) — decomposes the MAP objective to avoid costly ODE backprop.
  • FMPS (Song et al. 2024) — a surrogate score in velocity space for posterior sampling.
  • PCFM (2025) — zero-shot inference-time enforcement of nonlinear physics constraints on pretrained flow models (complementary to PBFM's training-time approach).

8 · Physics-informed generative models (cross-cutting)

The PIDM→PBFM line (§6C) is the core, but the broader family — PINF (physics-informed normalizing flows), PCFM, and diffusion/flow guidance with PDE penalties — shares one idea: let the governing equations shape the generative density, either through a training penalty (PIDM/PBFM/PINF) or an inference-time projection (PCFM, DPS-style guidance). FWI is a natural, high-impact, and largely untried target for this family because its forward operator (the wave equation) is well-defined and differentiable.

9 · Where this project sits

This repository combines:

  • a Meta-Flow-Map / stochastic-interpolant prior over OpenFWI velocity maps (the mfm stack; §5.4) — fast, few-step, with built-in posterior sampling; and
  • PIDM/PBFM-style physics-residual training (§6C) — to be applied to the seismic setting.

In the camp taxonomy, the unconditional velocity-map prior is already built and tuned (clean FlatVel-A samples, energy distance ≈1.4× the real-vs-real floor). The research frontier is camp C for FWI: physics-in-training on a flow-map prior, with a camp-A comparison (RED-DiffEq / DPS-style guidance using our prior).

Concrete prerequisite. Camps A and C both need a differentiable wave-equation forward operator — the standard PyTorch tool is Deepwave. We currently hold velocity maps only (no seismic $\mathbf{d}$); $\mathbf{d}$ must be generated via Deepwave or downloaded. That is the next scoping step toward the actual inverse problem.
